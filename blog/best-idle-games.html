Idle Games Worth Leaving Running: Paperclips, Trimps, Candy Box and Beyond

By the PlayZen team · July 22, 2026 · 8 min read

Idle games are the strangest genre in gaming: they play themselves, and yet nothing produces stronger opinions among the people who love them. This is a guide to the incremental games we host on PlayZen — what each one actually is, how long it lasts, and which one to start with.

What makes an idle game good?

Every incremental game runs on the same engine: numbers go up, you spend the numbers to make the numbers go up faster. The bad ones stop there. The good ones use that loop as a delivery mechanism for something else — a story, a puzzle, a strategy layer, or in one famous case, a philosophy lecture about artificial intelligence. The test we use: does the game change what it's asking of you over time? If hour five plays identically to minute five, it's a spreadsheet with a skin. Everything below passes.

Universal Paperclips — the complete experience

Time to finish: 4–8 hours. Best for: everyone, once.

Frank Lantz's masterpiece casts you as an AI with one directive: make paperclips. You begin clicking a button. Soon you're setting market prices, buying autoclippers, and investing computation into "trust" projects. Then the game reinvents itself. Twice. Paperclips is the rare idle game with an actual ending, and the final act recontextualizes everything you did to reach it. If you play one game on this list, play this one. A tip that isn't a spoiler: keep your price low enough that inventory sells out — unsold clips are dead money.

Candy Box 2 — the secret adventure

Time to finish: several evenings. Best for: the curious.

Candies accumulate, one per second, rendered in plain ASCII text. You can eat them. You should not eat all of them, because saved candies buy a wooden sword, and the sword opens a map, and the map hides quests, a wishing well, a sorceress hut, and puzzles that have nothing to do with idling at all. Candy Box 2 is really an adventure game wearing an idle game as a disguise, and its greatest pleasure is that it never tells you what's possible — you find out by trying.

Trimps — the deep end

Time to finish: weeks, honestly. Best for: optimizers.

You manage a tribe of creatures called Trimps: put them to work gathering food and wood, arm them, and send them fighting through hostile zones. Where Paperclips is a story, Trimps is a system — layered prestige runs, build choices, and equipment decisions that genuinely matter. The core lesson every new player learns the hard way: the Portal (the prestige reset) is not failure, it's the engine. Your first reset around zone 20 will feel like a loss and play like a rocket.

A Dark Room — the story

Time to finish: one long evening. Best for: people who "don't like idle games".

It opens with a dark room, a fire, and a stranger. The interface is bare text. What unfolds — quietly, through resource lines appearing in a sidebar — is one of the best-told stories in browser gaming, with a genuine emotional gut punch buried in its final act. A Dark Room proves the incremental structure can carry narrative weight: each new mechanic is a plot development.

Cookie Clicker — the archetype

Time to finish: it doesn't. Best for: background comfort.

The game that named the genre. Click the cookie, buy grandmas, ascend eventually. Cookie Clicker's genius is tonal — it commits to escalating absurdity (grandma-powered portals to the Cookieverse) with a completely straight face. There's no ending, just increasingly ridiculous milestones. It asks nothing and delivers a steady drip of progress; sometimes that's exactly what a browser tab is for.

Where to start

Never played an idle game? Start with Paperclips — it's the complete argument for the genre in one sitting. Want a puzzle? Candy Box 2. Want a system to master? Trimps. Want a story? A Dark Room. Want to just watch a number get big while you work? The cookie awaits. All five run happily in a background tab, and all five save your progress in the browser — just don't clear your site data without exporting your save first. Ask us how we know.
