Sudoku Techniques: From Your First Grid to Genuinely Hard Puzzles

By the PlayZen team · July 22, 2026 · 8 min read

Every sudoku, from gentle to diabolical, is solved with the same promise: there is always a next logical step, and you never have to guess. The difference between a beginner and a strong solver is simply the size of their toolbox when the easy steps run out. Here's the toolbox, in the order you should learn it — then open a fresh grid and put it to work.

Rule zero: scan before you stare

Weak solvers stare at one stubborn cell. Strong solvers sweep the whole grid cheaply first. Start every puzzle the same way: pick the most common digit among the givens and check where it can live in each box; then do the same for the next digit. This "cross-hatching" sweep fills the free squares fast and — more importantly — builds the mental map everything else depends on.

Level 1: singles

Naked single: a cell where eight digits are already excluded by its row, column, and box — only one candidate remains. Hidden single: a digit that has only one possible home within a row, column, or box, even though that cell could hold other numbers. Hidden singles are the workhorse of easy and medium puzzles; most grids labeled "hard" still fall to a patient, complete singles sweep. If you take one habit from this article: every time you place a digit, immediately re-check the three units (row, column, box) that placement touches. Placements cascade.

Level 2: pencil marks, done properly

When singles dry up, write candidates in the corners — but only for the digits you're actively working, not all nine everywhere. Full-grid pencil marking front-loads twenty minutes of clerical work and buries the signal in noise. Mark two or three digits, exhaust them, move on. On paper this discipline saves your eraser; in a browser grid it saves your patience.

Level 3: pairs and their consequences

Naked pair: two cells in the same unit that each hold exactly the same two candidates — those two digits are spoken for, so erase them from every other cell in the unit. Hidden pair: two digits that only appear in the same two cells of a unit; whatever else those cells were considering, they're now a private club of two. Pointing pair: when a digit's only spots in a box line up in one row or column, that digit is banished from the rest of that row or column outside the box. These three eliminations crack most puzzles labeled "hard", and they're all the same idea wearing different hats: candidates that must be somewhere are forbidden everywhere else.

Level 4: the X-wing and friends

When a digit appears in exactly two columns of one row, and the same two columns of another row, it forms a rectangle — an X-wing — and that digit can be eliminated from those two columns everywhere else. It sounds exotic; on the grid it's just the pointing-pair logic stretched across the whole board. Genuinely expert techniques (swordfish, XY-wings, coloring chains) continue the same escalation, but be honest with yourself: puzzles requiring them are rare outside championship sets. Master levels one through three and you will finish 95% of everything published.

The habits that actually make you faster

Speed in sudoku isn't hurrying — it's not re-deriving what you already know. Three habits compound: work digits, not cells (sweep one number across the grid rather than interrogating one square); trust the no-guess promise (the moment you feel like guessing, there's a hidden single you haven't found — usually in the most crowded box); and finish your chains (when a placement triggers another, follow the cascade to its end before starting anything new). Twenty minutes a day for two weeks and the medium grids that used to take half an hour fall in eight.
